The Keeper of Gull Point

Every lighthouse has a story, but the one at Gull Point has more than most. It stands on a narrow spit of granite at the far end of a fishing town that most maps forget to name. The tower is sixty-two feet tall, painted white with a single red band, and for one hundred and fourteen years it has turned its slow beam across the water every night without fail. Tonight, we are going to climb its spiral stairs, meet the people who kept it burning, and find out why a small town still gathers on the rocks every autumn to watch the light come on.

Let's begin at the bottom, with the stone.

The builders arrived in the spring of 1910. There were eleven of them, led by a quiet engineer named Thomas Avery, who had spent most of his career designing bridges and had never once built anything meant to stand against the open sea. He was, by his own account, terrified. In a letter to his sister, he wrote: "The ocean here does not argue. It simply waits, and it is very patient." Avery had good reason to worry. The point was battered by storms from the northeast for half the year, and the closest quarry was forty miles inland. Every block of granite had to come by wagon, then by barge, then by hand.

The work went slowly. In the first summer, the crew finished only the foundation, a ring of stone sunk nine feet into the bedrock. In the second summer, a gale tore away the scaffolding twice, and one of the masons, a young man named Peter Lund, broke his arm in the fall. He came back three weeks later with his arm in a sling and spent the rest of the season mixing mortar with one hand. When the townspeople asked him why he had returned, he said only that he wanted to see the light lit. He did. On the evening of October 3, 1912, the lamp was filled with oil, the wick was trimmed, and the beam swept across Gull Point for the very first time.

The original lens was a marvel. It was a third-order Fresnel lens, shipped from Paris in four wooden crates packed with straw. Its glass prisms were arranged in rings, each one angled to bend the light from a single flame into a beam that could be seen almost twenty miles out to sea. The whole assembly weighed more than a thousand pounds, and yet it floated on a bath of mercury so smoothly that a child could turn it with one finger. A clockwork mechanism, wound by hand every four hours, kept it rotating at exactly one turn every thirty seconds. That rhythm, a flash every half minute, became the lighthouse's signature. Sailors called it the heartbeat of the coast.

Now, let's meet the keepers.

The first keeper of Gull Point was a former ship's carpenter named Elias Brand. He arrived with his wife, Margaret, their two daughters, a goat, and a crate of books. The keeper's house was a small, square cottage at the foot of the tower, with thick walls and a kitchen stove that never quite drew properly. The job was simple to describe and exhausting to do. Light the lamp at sunset. Wind the clockwork every four hours. Trim the wick, polish the brass, clean the soot from the glass. Record the weather in the logbook. Put the lamp out at sunrise. Sleep, if possible. Repeat, every single day of the year.

Elias kept the light for twenty-three years, and his logbooks still survive. They are mostly lists: wind direction, visibility, the number of ships that passed. But every so often he wrote something more. On one winter night in 1919 he noted, in very small handwriting, that the snow was falling so thickly he could not see the base of his own tower. He climbed the stairs every hour until morning to make sure the lens was clear. At the bottom of the page, he added a single line: "Lamp held. All souls safe, so far as I know."

That phrase, so far as I know, says a great deal about the job. A keeper rarely learned whether the light had helped anyone. Ships passed in the dark and were gone by morning. Sometimes a captain would send a letter, or a fisherman would leave a basket of fish on the cottage step without a word. Mostly, though, there was only silence, and the steady turning of the light.

Margaret Brand was, in many ways, the second keeper. She learned to trim the wick and wind the clockwork within the first month, and when Elias fell ill with pneumonia in the winter of 1924, she kept the light alone for six weeks. She was not paid for it. The official records list only her husband's name. But the logbook tells the truth. For those six weeks, the handwriting changes. It becomes neater, smaller, and slightly slanted to the left, and the entries become longer. She described the color of the sky at dawn, the sound of ice cracking on the rocks, and the way the beam looked from the kitchen window when she went down to heat water for tea. On the last day before Elias recovered, she wrote: "He is better. I find I am almost sorry. The light and I were getting along."

When Elias retired in 1935, the job passed to a man named Walter Kincaid, and with him came the first great change. In 1938, the oil lamp was replaced with an electric bulb. The townspeople were divided. Some were thrilled; the electric light was brighter, cleaner, and far less work. Others felt something had been lost. Walter himself admitted that he missed the smell of the oil and the small ritual of trimming the wick. "A bulb," he told a reporter from the county newspaper, "does not need you. A flame does."

Still, the work went on. Electricity failed often in those years, especially during storms, which was exactly when the light mattered most. Walter kept a backup oil lamp ready at all times, and more than once he found himself racing up the stairs in the dark, matches in his teeth, to get a flame going before the next ship came round the point. He later estimated that he had relit the old lamp by hand at least forty times.

Then came the war years. From 1942 to 1945, the light at Gull Point was dimmed, and on some nights it was turned off entirely, to avoid guiding enemy submarines along the coast. It was a strange and difficult time for the keeper. For the first time, his job was not to keep the light burning but to keep it dark, and to listen. Walter and two volunteers from the town took turns in the lantern room with binoculars and a telephone, watching the water for anything unusual. Most nights, they saw nothing at all. One night in March of 1943, they saw a light where there should not have been one, far out past the reef. They reported it, and it was never explained. Walter wrote about it only once, years later, and said simply that he hoped it had been a fishing boat that had lost its way and found it again.

After the war, the light came back to full strength, and the town grew. A new road was built along the shore. Summer visitors began to arrive, and many of them wanted to climb the tower. Walter, who was not a natural host, found himself giving tours every Saturday afternoon. He was gruff at first, but the children won him over. He let them wind the old clockwork, which by then was kept only for show, and he told them stories about storms and shipwrecks, most of which were true. A woman who took the tour as a girl in 1951 remembered it seventy years later. "He let me look through the lens," she said. "The whole sea turned upside down. I have never forgotten it."

The third keeper, and the last, was Ruth Okafor, who took over in 1962. She was the first woman ever officially appointed to the post, and the local paper made a great fuss about it. Ruth did not. When asked how she felt about making history, she replied that she was mostly worried about the leak in the lantern room roof. She fixed the leak herself, over the course of a long summer, and then turned her attention to everything else that needed doing. She repainted the tower, replanted the garden, and rebuilt the stone wall that kept the sea out of the cottage cellar during spring tides.

Ruth was also a meticulous record keeper, and her logbooks are the most detailed of all. She tracked the weather, of course, but she also recorded the birds that nested on the point, the dates of the first frost and the last, and the names of every ship she could identify. Over twenty-one years, her list grew to more than four thousand entries. It is now kept in the county archive, and researchers still use it to study how the coastline has changed.

But Ruth is best remembered for something else. In the autumn of 1969, a sudden storm caught a small fishing boat, the Mary Ellen, just off the reef. The boat's engine had failed, and its radio was dead. Its crew of three, a father and his two sons, were drifting toward the rocks in the dark. Ruth saw their flare at about two in the morning. The town had no lifeboat of its own, and the nearest rescue station was more than an hour away. So she did the only thing she could think of. She stopped the lens from turning.

That may sound like a small thing, but it was not. A rotating light flashes; it tells a sailor where the lighthouse is, but only for an instant at a time. By locking the lens in place, Ruth turned the flashing beacon into a steady beam, and she aimed it directly at the gap in the reef, the one safe channel through the rocks. Then she stood in the lantern room and held it there, adjusting it by hand as the wind shifted, for almost three hours. The crew of the Mary Ellen saw the beam, understood what it meant, and rowed for it. They came through the gap just before dawn, soaked and exhausted, and every one of them alive.

The father, a man named Daniel Reyes, came to the lighthouse the next day to thank her. Ruth, by all accounts, was embarrassed. She made him a cup of tea and asked him about his engine. Years later, his younger son, by then a grandfather himself, said that his family still told the story every autumn. "We call it the night the light stood still," he said. "My father used to say that for three hours, that lighthouse was looking only at us."

Ruth retired in 1983, and the light was automated the same year. A small electronic controller now switches it on at dusk and off at dawn, and a sensor reports any fault to an office in the city. No one has lived in the keeper's cottage since. For a few years, the building stood empty, and the town worried that the whole place would be sold or simply left to fall apart. Then, in 1991, a group of volunteers formed the Gull Point Lighthouse Society. They raised money with bake sales, raffles, and an annual swim across the harbor that grew more popular every year. Slowly, they restored the cottage, rebuilt the garden wall once again, and turned the old kitchen into a small museum.

The museum is modest, but it is wonderful. In one glass case, you can see Elias Brand's logbooks, open to the page from the winter of 1919. In another, you can see Margaret's six weeks of neat, slanted handwriting. There is the backup oil lamp that Walter Kincaid relit forty times, still black with soot. There is a photograph of Ruth Okafor on the roof of the lantern room, a hammer in one hand and a coffee mug in the other, looking faintly annoyed at the photographer. And there, in the center of the room, is the original Fresnel lens, retired from service in 1983 and now resting on a wooden stand, its prisms still catching the light from the window.

Before we leave the museum, there is one more object to see, and you will probably hear about it before you see it. In the corner, hanging from a heavy oak frame, is the old fog bell. Long before the light was automated, and long before radar, the bell was the lighthouse's second voice. On clear nights, the beam did the work. But on foggy nights, when the light could barely reach the end of the point, the keeper rang the bell instead, two strokes every twenty seconds, for as long as the fog lasted.

That could mean a very long time. The logbooks record one fog in the summer of 1927 that lasted for fifty-one hours. Elias and Margaret took turns at the bell rope in four-hour shifts, and by the end, Elias wrote, his shoulders felt as if they belonged to someone much older. In 1931, a mechanical striker was installed, driven by a falling weight like a giant clock, and it had to be wound by hand every two hours. Walter Kincaid hated it. He said the striker was always either a little too fast or a little too slow, and that the ships could tell the difference. Whether that was true, no one can say. But he kept a small notebook of adjustments, and the entries continue, in his careful handwriting, for almost twenty years.

The bell was finally silenced in 1975, when an electronic horn took its place. The horn was louder and more reliable, and the town hated it immediately. People said it sounded like a cow with a cold. Ruth Okafor, who was keeper at the time, quietly agreed. Today, the volunteers ring the old bell exactly once a year, at the end of the autumn gathering, two strokes, just as it used to sound. Children are allowed to pull the rope. The sound carries across the whole harbor, low and round and strangely comforting, and more than one visitor has said that it sounds like the town remembering something.

So why does the town still gather on the rocks every autumn?

It started as a small thing. On the first anniversary of the Mary Ellen rescue, Daniel Reyes and his sons walked out to the point at sunset to watch the light come on. The next year, a few of their neighbors joined them. Within a decade, it had become a tradition. Today, on the first Saturday of October, several hundred people gather on the granite at Gull Point. They bring blankets and thermoses, and the children run along the rocks until the sun starts to sink. Then, as the sky turns orange and then violet, everyone falls quiet. Someone reads a few lines from the old logbooks. Someone else reads the names of the keepers: Elias and Margaret Brand, Walter Kincaid, Ruth Okafor. And at the moment the light switches on, the whole crowd turns to watch its first slow sweep across the water.

Nobody gives a speech. There is nothing that really needs to be said. The light comes on, the way it has every night for more than a century, and for a moment the people of the town stand together and look out at the dark sea, where somewhere, perhaps, a small boat is finding its way home.

There is one last detail worth mentioning. When the volunteers restored the keeper's cottage, they found something tucked behind a loose stone in the kitchen wall, next to the stove that never drew properly. It was a folded piece of paper, yellow with age. On it, in small, neat handwriting that slanted slightly to the left, someone had written a single sentence. Experts believe it was Margaret Brand, and that she hid it there sometime in the 1920s. It reads: "To whoever keeps this light after us: you will not often know whom you have saved, but keep it burning anyway."

The note is in the museum now, in its own small frame beside the lens. Most visitors walk past it without stopping. But the ones who do stop tend to stay for a long time.

And that is the story of the keeper of Gull Point, or rather, of all its keepers, the ones whose names are in the records and the ones whose names are not. The light is still turning tonight. If you are ever on that stretch of coast after dark, look for it. One flash every thirty seconds, steady as a heartbeat. Someone, a long time ago, decided it was worth keeping.
